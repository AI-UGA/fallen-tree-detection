Fallen Wood After Hurricane Helene, Measured from the Air

A UAV inventory of coarse woody debris
Dang Hoang, Rishab Subramaniyan, Owen Norman, Joey Hattan, Roger C. Lowe III
Interactive map, data and code

Background

What Helene left behind: trunks lying under the trees that survived (left), and a stand flattened in the open (right).

In September 2024, Hurricane Helene knocked down large stretches of Georgia forest. That fallen timber, called coarse woody debris, is fuel for the next wildfire, it decides where salvage crews go, and it holds carbon. Foresters usually measure it by walking survey lines. That gives a good average for a site, but it can't show where the wood actually is. A drone map can, as long as a computer finds and measures every piece.

Objectives

  1. Find every piece of fallen wood in the drone images.
  2. Measure each piece and sort trunks from fragments.
  3. Turn it all into a map land managers can use.

Methods: A Geospatial Workflow

Orthomosaic raster

The stitched drone map: 0.0508 m pixels, in UTM zone 17N.

Instance masks

The model draws an outline around every fallen piece.

Vector features

Each outline becomes a shape with its own length, width, volume and class: full stem or fragment.

Zonal statistics

We add up the pieces and volume inside each 127 m (1.6 ha) square, like this one.

Every piece ends up on the map with its own measurements. Each one is placed by its center, and lengths are in real meters. A full stem is 6.78 m or longer and passes the width and elongation rules. We set aside 2,515 detections: 735 cut off at tile edges, 1,084 too narrow, 695 too short and 1 not elongated.

Conclusions

One drone survey gave us a piece-by-piece count of the fallen wood across 478.7 ha. Full stems are only a quarter of the pieces but hold most of the volume, and the wood is bunched into a few hot spots. That hands land managers a short list of places to check first for fire risk, salvage and carbon. The trained model can also be run again soon after the next storm. Next, we want to raise its accuracy and make it work on other sites, sensors and kinds of imagery.
